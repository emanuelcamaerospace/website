Overview

Why a low-power laser?

Cheap commercial drones have created real security and privacy risks, especially in urban areas and conflict zones. The usual counter-measures — kinetic interception or RF jamming — are expensive per engagement, legally constrained, or unsafe around people.

High-energy laser systems solve some of this, but they need kilowatt-class power to destroy the airframe. This thesis takes a different route: blind the drone's camera instead of destroying the drone. A much weaker laser is enough, provided it stays on the sensor long enough.

That turns the problem into one of pointing precision. A vision system estimates the target's position and velocity; those estimates drive a two-axis gimbal carrying the laser, and how well it stays aligned determines the achieved Time on Target (TOT). Through dynamic modelling, control design, simulation and field testing, the work evaluates whether the concept holds up in realistic scenarios — including evasive targets.

Key figure of merit

Time on Target

How long the laser spot stays on the target's optical sensor during an engagement. Every modelling and control choice in the thesis is judged by its effect on TOT across nominal and evasive trajectories.

01 · CONTROL

GA-optimised Integral Sliding Mode Control

An ISMC scheme on a precision 2-axis gimbal, with gains tuned by a genetic algorithm for robust visual tracking under disturbances.

02 · SIMULATION

End-to-end Simulink framework

High-fidelity gimbal dynamics, visual detection pipeline, sensor-noise models and target trajectory physics in one loop, before any hardware.

03 · HARDWARE

Lightweight C-UAS prototype

Custom 3D-printed mechanics, Pixhawk-based embedded computing, servo actuators and dedicated power regulation.

04 · VALIDATION

Field experiments

Physical tests of optical tracking and neutralisation, establishing baseline metrics for mobile and interceptor-drone deployments.

System architecture

From pixels to photons

A closed loop running continuously: every new camera frame updates the target estimate, the controller corrects the gimbal, and the laser's alignment is what decides the engagement.

Detect

Camera and vision pipeline locate the drone in each frame.

Estimate

Target position and velocity estimated from noisy measurements.

Control

GA-tuned ISMC computes yaw and pitch torque commands.

Actuate

Pixhawk drives the servo-actuated 2-axis gimbal.

Engage

Laser held on the optical sensor — Time on Target.

Loop closes on every camera frame

Modelling & simulation

Gimbal dynamics and control

The yaw–pitch gimbal was derived with Newton–Euler, Lagrange–Euler and Kane's method, which give identical equations and isolate the gyroscopic and inertial coupling as disturbance torques. The controller is then designed to reject exactly those disturbances.

Hardware

The prototype

A lightweight platform designed in Siemens NX to take the controller out of simulation: 3D-printed gimbal structure, Pixhawk-based embedded control, servo actuators and a dedicated power-regulation stage.

Results

Live hardware test with a 7 W laser model

The prototype tracked a moving hand target at roughly 1 m for a 20 s run. The recorded tracking data were then fed into the laser exposure model for a 7 W laser and a camera sensor of 10 mm radius.

Time on target · 10 mm-radius sensor 16.8% 3.36 s of the 20 s run with the beam on a sensor of 10 mm radius
Cumulative fluence 6.37J/cm² Above the modelled damage threshold of about 2 J/cm²
Time to sensor damage ≈ 8.5s From the start of the run, at ≈ 1 m range
Median tracking error < 1° 0.93° yaw · 0.64° pitch (IMU-measured vs desired)
