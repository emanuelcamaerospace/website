Laser-based interception of unmanned aerial systems

A lower-power alternative to laser air defence, neutralising the optical sensors of a UAV with a precisely aimed laser. This shifts the main technical challenge to tracking and pointing precision.

Overview

Why a low-power laser?

Low-cost commercial UAVs pose significant privacy and security challenges. Kinetic interception and radio-frequency jamming are often unsuitable for urban environments due to collateral risks and cost, while high-power laser systems are expensive and complex.

Current laser defence systems destroy the structure of the target with kilowatt-class lasers over several kilometres. This thesis proposes lower-power lasers that target the optical sensors of the drone at shorter ranges. The reduced range is offset by the compact size and low weight of the laser, which allow it to be mounted on flexible platforms such as an interceptor drone.

A visual detection system estimates the trajectory of the UAV and drives a gimbal-mounted laser. Interception success is directly tied to the time on target, since sustained irradiation increases the energy delivered to the sensor.

Research objectives

  • Simulate the integrated system, from visual detection to laser energy delivery.
  • Design an integral sliding mode controller and tune it by optimisation, in simulation and on hardware.
  • Build a prototype and measure its tracking error against video of a UAV at about 3 m.
  • Estimate the time on target and the laser power needed to blind or damage the sensor.

System architecture

From pixels to photons

Most interception systems comprise two components, the detection of the target and the control of the interception mechanism. Here, a visual detection system tracks the UAV and feeds its estimated position to the controller of a two-axis gimbal carrying the laser.

Detect

A YOLO-based vision system locates the drone in each frame.

Estimate

A Kalman filter estimates the target position from noisy detections.

Control

The integral sliding mode controller computes the yaw and pitch commands.

Actuate

Servo actuators drive the two-axis gimbal.

Engage

Once aligned, the laser irradiates the camera sensor.

Loop closes on every camera frame

Modelling & simulation

Gimbal dynamics and control

The gimbal dynamics were derived with the Newton-Euler, Lagrange-Euler and Kane methods, which give the same equations. A dynamic model of the gimbal is combined with an integral sliding mode controller, and the whole system is simulated in Simulink before hardware implementation.

Hardware

The prototype

A two-axis gimbal prototype was built from hobby-grade components and 3D-printed parts. The controller runs in Simulink on a host computer, and its parameters were optimised on the prototype by a genetic algorithm.

Results

How the results were obtained

On recorded footage of a UAV at about 3 m, median tracking errors were 0.56° in yaw and 0.29° in pitch, limited mainly by the low-cost servos. In a live test, the prototype tracked a hand at about 1 m for 20 s. The tracking data were then used in the laser energy model with a 7 W laser and a sensor of 10 mm radius.

These results indicate that, at short range, pointing precision rather than laser power is the limiting factor. They motivate upgraded actuators and mobile platforms such as an interceptor drone.

Part of this work is carried out with the Portuguese Air Force. Some details and media are published only once cleared.
